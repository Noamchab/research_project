Why Comedy?

Since Antiquity, theater has been divided into two main genres: comedy and tragedy. Philosophers and critics traditionally considered tragedy the "noble" genre, while comedy was viewed as lighter, sometimes inferior entertainment.

This project explores this cultural hierarchy not through ancient theater, but through modern cinema, analyzing whether comedy is still underestimated compared to tragedy.

Method

To classify each film as a comedy, a tragedy, or neither, we use the Neoclassical definitions of comedy and tragedy in drama and provide them to an LLM.

Here are the definitions we mean by comedy and tragedy:

Comedy (Neoclassical)

Comedy in the Neoclassical period typically featured ordinary or middle-class characters, not nobles or kings. It aimed to entertain and to correct manners through laughter. The plot often relied on misunderstandings, social satire, and ridicule of human weaknesses. Most importantly, comedy always ended with a happy resolution, often involving marriages or restored harmony.

Tragedy (Neoclassical)

Tragedy was considered the highest genre, focusing on noble characters such as kings, queens, or heroes. It depicted inescapable conflicts, destructive passions, and fate leading to inevitable downfall. The aim was to inspire awe and provoke catharsis in the audience through feelings of pity and fear.
